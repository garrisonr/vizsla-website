Tested hardware

The machines and gear Vizsla is tested on

No lab, no review units — these are the computers on the desks where Vizsla is built, and the exact peripherals plugged into them, read off the machines themselves on 13 November 2026. If a thing isn’t listed here, we haven’t lived with it, and we’d rather leave the space blank than guess.

The test units

Three machines carry the whole fleet’s testing. They are named here by shape, not by brand — what matters is the machinery inside.

Test Unit 1 — the towerTest Unit 2 — the miniTest Unit 3 — the small desktop
Processor8-core / 16-thread desktop processor, current generation (Zen 5), boosting to 5.58 GHz4-core low-power mobile-class processor (N-series), up to 3.6 GHz14-core / 28-thread workstation processor (v4 generation), 2.4 GHz base, 3.3 GHz boost
Memory32 GB DDR5, running at 5200 MT/s12 GB LPDDR5, soldered, running at 4800 MT/s32 GB DDR4 ECC, 2400 MT/s
Storage insideOne ≈1 TB NVMe SSD, holding the systemTwo ≈2 TB NVMe SSDs (the system on one), plus ≈64 GB eMMCOne ≈2 TB NVMe SSD holding the system, plus one ≈960 GB SATA SSD

The mini is the honest floor of the fleet: if Vizsla runs well there — twelve gigabytes of memory and a low-power processor — it runs well anywhere it belongs.

What a machine needs

The installer’s targets, plainly:

  • Installer media: a USB stick of at least 128 GB.
  • The install drive: an SSD or NVMe drive of at least 256 GB — and 1 TB is the minimum we recommend for comfortable, optimal use.
  • Memory: at least 12 GB of RAM — the smallest test unit above runs on exactly that.
  • Hard drives: SATA hard drives and external hard drives are recommended for media storage and backup only — never as the drive Vizsla itself is installed on. Spinning disks are where the films and the backups live; the system lives on flash.

GPS receivers

Vizsla’s maps and dash faces take their position from USB GPS receivers, read through gpsd — the system never phones a location home. Two receivers are in daily testing, one per chipset family, on purpose:

  • GlobalSat BU-353N — a multi-GNSS puck (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou) on the mini. About $50 when bought in October 2026. A small honesty: its USB name tag only admits to being a serial bridge — the model is named by the purchase record and the fleet’s own configuration, and its live fix is verified nightly.
  • VK-162 G-Mouse — a u-blox 7 receiver on the tower, on a cable long enough that the puck can sit in a window for a clear sky while the computer stays at the desk. About $17 when bought in November 2026.

Webcam

  • Logitech Brio 100 — the tower’s camera: a 1080p-class USB webcam that speaks the standard UVC language, so it works with no drivers and no account.

The TV remote

  • G10S Pro BT — an air-mouse remote with a microphone, about $22 when bought in November 2026. In this house it is tested through its 2.4 GHz USB dongle: the keys, the air mouse, and the microphone all arrive as ordinary USB devices, and its voice button has been capture-verified through that microphone. Honestly stated: it has never been run here over Bluetooth — on these machines it is a dongle remote, and that is the setup we can vouch for.

Fingerprint reader

  • DigitalPersona U.are.U 4500 — the reader chosen for Secrets’ fingerprint unlock, from the fingerprint library’s own supported list. It arrives Sunday 15 November 2026; its listing here is ahead of its first test, and we say so — the enrollment session is the test, and this line gets its verdict after it.

Game controller

A controller belongs on this list — the Arcade is in the box, and the dash wants one too. The space is deliberately blank for now: a few candidates are named in our notes, none has been bought and lived with yet, and this page only names what we have tested. When one earns the spot, it will appear here with the same plain treatment as everything above.
