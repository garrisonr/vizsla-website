Phone & texting

A real phone number, in the system

Message apps are welcome here — and so is an actual telephone line, with calling and texting, running natively in Vizsla’s own phone app.

You may use Facebook Messenger, WhatsApp, Signal, or Telegram in Vizsla OS, the way you would anywhere. But you can also have traditional phone service — a real phone number that rings, sends and receives texts, and takes voicemail — by bringing a VoIP/SIP provider of your own. Vizsla’s phone app, Elefunk, is built for exactly that: you bring the line, Elefunk is the phone.

There are two honest ways to do it. One is free and web-only. The other is a few dollars of setup and pennies of use, and it is the one this house actually runs on.

The free way: Google Voice, in the web app

Google Voice is the free example, and it mostly works like a traditional phone carrier: a real number, calls, texts, voicemail. Plenty of people will stop right there and be perfectly happy.

The honest limit: there is no direct Vizsla OS app integration for Google Voice. Google doesn’t offer the kind of open access Elefunk needs, so on Vizsla it is web-app calling and texting only — you use it in the browser, not inside the system’s own phone app. If you want your calls, texts, and voicemail living natively beside your mail and calendar, you want a provider with an API. That’s the next section.

The native way: a provider with an API — Telnyx, field-tested

Telnyx is a phone carrier that also offers full API access, which is what lets a native app be a real phone. It is the provider Elefunk is field-tested against in this house, in daily use: calls, texts, voicemail, and caller ID, all inside Elefunk — no browser, no separate phone app, no carrier contract. You pay the carrier directly, at published pay-as-you-go rates; Vizsla takes no cut and charges nothing for the phone app.

Four steps, start to ringing:

  • Step 1 — Create your account and verify it’s you

    Sign up with Telnyx and complete its identity verification (the requirements list below is the whole of it — a cell phone for two-factor codes, a photo of your government-issued ID, and a GitHub or LinkedIn account that has already verified you).

  • Step 2 — Rent a local phone number

    Pick a local number in your area code. It rents for $1 a month, and it is yours for as long as you keep it.

  • Step 3 — Register the number for texting (10DLC)

    US carriers require application-to-person texting on local numbers to be registered — the system is called 10DLC. Publish a privacy policy that includes opt-out instructions, then register your brand and campaign with a physical mailing address that matches your government-issued ID (a P.O. Box won’t do). The registry fees are passed through at cost. Here is the actual privacy policy running on this house’s own field-test line, if you want to see what one looks like.

  • Step 4 — Connect the provider in Elefunk

    Hand Elefunk the connection details once, and the line is live: make and receive calls, send and receive texts, get voicemail with transcripts, and tap Identify on an unknown caller to see what the carrier and the public spam reports know about the number.

What it costs

Every rate below was read on Telnyx’s own pricing pages on 13 November 2026, in US dollars. Telnyx is pay-as-you-go: no plan, no seats, no subscription — you keep a balance and the meters below are what runs against it.

The meterHow it runsPublished rate
Your phone number A local US number, rented month to month. $1.00 / month
Calls you make US local calls, billed in whole minutes with a one-minute minimum — a 20-second call bills as a minute. from $0.005 / min
Calls you receive Same whole-minute billing on calls to your local number. from $0.0032 / min
Texts (SMS) Charged per message part (a long text is more than one part), sent or received, plus the receiving carrier’s fee of roughly $0.0035–$0.005 per part at the major US carriers. $0.004 / part + carrier fee
Picture texts (MMS) Per message part plus carrier fee (roughly $0.007–$0.01 per part outbound at the major carriers). $0.015 out · $0.005 in / part
Caller-ID lookup Elefunk’s Identify asks the carrier for the number’s carrier and caller name in one dip. Elefunk caches the answer per caller, so you pay once per number — not per call. $0.0045–$0.007 / lookup
Texting registration (10DLC) One-time registry fees, passed through at cost: $4.50 to register your brand and $15 for the campaign review. A sole-proprietor campaign then runs $2 a month. Standard business campaigns run more ($1.50–$10 a month). ≈$19.50 once + $2 / mo

What getting started actually cost

Published rates are the math; here is the reality. The first statement on this house’s field-test account, all-in:

10DLC one-time setup — $19.00
Local phone number — $1.67 (pro-rated first month, plus the one-time setup)
SMS — $0.07 (pro-rated)
HD Voice — $0.00
Total charges: $20.74

Those are pro-rated first-bill amounts, not per-unit rates — the first month is a partial month. Ongoing, the fixed cost of the line is the $1 number and the $2 campaign fee; everything else is the pennies in the table, only when you use them.

What you’ll need

Telnyx verifies people before it hands them a carrier line, and US carriers require the texting registration. From our own field test, have these ready:

  • A cellular phone (not a VoIP number) that can receive two-factor authentication codes.
  • A photograph of your government-issued ID for identity verification.
  • Identity verification through GitHub or LinkedIn — the account must already be verified by GitHub or LinkedIn before Telnyx will accept it as a verification source, so an account you made yesterday won’t do.
  • A published privacy policy with opt-out instructions for your texting program (Step 3 above).
  • A physical mailing address that matches your government-issued ID — not a P.O. Box.
  • A way to pay — Telnyx runs on a prepaid balance; the line meters against it.

One boundary, said plainly: Elefunk is not an emergency service. For 911, use a regular phone line — the same rule the app itself states on its face.
