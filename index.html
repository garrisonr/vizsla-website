Vizsla OS

Private by design, kind on purpose.

Papers, mail, messages, and ads, all flying at you at once — Vizsla brings them to one calm place. That’s the whole idea.

The honest status: Vizsla OS is in active development, being built in the open at home. There is no download yet — when there is, it will be right here. Read how it’s being made.

See what’s inside Read the story

ArrivalTODAY, IN ORDER

Vizsla OS has been developed over months in Leesburg, Florida, by Richard Garrison — an HR and healthcare-operations man with a degree in English and Anthropology, a head full of whole worlds, and no intention of waiting a decade — working with an AI he directs in plain language, often sixteen hours a day. The building took months; the ideas were already years old — listed, wished for, and blueprinted on paper since at least 2019, with the full plan for that system written in the summer of 2026. Nothing requires an account. It is deliberately different in all the ways that matter — not just privacy, but kindness — and it is a labor of love that is still listening.

Open, too: Vizsla OS runs on the Linux kernel, and the whole project is free and open-source software — yours to modify and redistribute. The open-source statement.

What’s new

New in November 2026 — shipped, in the house, and in daily use. Vizsla itself is still not released; this is the work arriving, piece by piece.

Sniff Search

It forgives typos now

Type “pohtos” and Sniff finds your photos — and shows its guess out loud, with one tap to search exactly what you typed. No silent corrections, no dead ends.

Vizsla Arcade

Shark Supper, repainted

The shark game surfaced with a whole new painted cast — Bagel the beagle in full scuba gear included — and passed its owner’s own playtest. Word Wheel’s board finally fits its stage, too.

AudioWaves

The MIDI shelf is open

The family’s MIDI soundtracks — the Arcade songbook and the theme tunes — now sit in the music library next to everything else, playable like any record in the house.

A sneak peek

Cool features are better shown than described — so here are four of the house’s apps as they actually look this November. No mockups anywhere on this site: these are real captures of the real software.

Calm by default — ready when you are

The default here is calm: a quiet screen, nothing pulsing for your attention. But calm is a posture, not a limit.

Suit and tie, one click

When it’s time to work, Vizsla puts on a virtual suit and tie and gets to business — your workspaces, documents, and tools up front, with a click of a button. Manners stay on; the tie is optional for you.

Rock all night

Or don’t work at all. Play your favorite tunes all night, or play virtually any video game — the arcade cabinet and the classic consoles are already in the house.

It teaches — then hushes

Vizsla teaches you how to do things as you go, in plain words, in its own manual and in the moment. And when you’d rather it didn’t, you can tell it to shut up — and it does. Kindly.

One piece of it is already loose in the wild: System Profiler, a Vizsla app you can download and run on any Linux PC today. Try it now.

Who Vizsla OS isn’t for

Honesty is a feature here, so here it is up front: Vizsla is opinionated, and opinionated software is wrong for somebody. If you’re on this list, we’d rather tell you now than after you’ve rearranged your desk.

  • Large businesses

    Not yet, anyway. Vizsla is built for households and small shops. A company that needs fleets deployed, audits passed, and contracts signed should check back later.

  • Schools

    Not yet, anyway. The classroom machinery — student accounts, teacher controls, rooms full of identical laptops — is on the roadmap, not in the box.

  • People who need rock-solid stability

    Coming soon-ish. Vizsla is young and moving fast, and it changes under you on purpose. If your machine must never, ever surprise you, let it grow up a little first.

  • People with very old PCs

    Not going to happen. Vizsla is generous, not nostalgic — it expects a reasonably modern machine and spends its effort there.

  • ARM processors and M-series Macs

    Eventually, hopefully. Today Vizsla runs on x86-64 PCs. The door is open; the build isn’t built.

  • People who want a very minimal operating system

    Vizsla is intentionally very, very maximal — everything in the box, in the best way. It ships in three editions — Core, Plus, and Fully Loaded — and none of them is the small one. If a bare desktop and two apps is your idea of peace, this will feel like a parade.

  • People who need an enterprise support contract

    A vendor to call at 3 AM, and someone to sue when it breaks. Vizsla has a person, not a call center — a kind one, who answers in plain words, but a person.

  • Competitive gamers on kernel-level anti-cheat

    “Virtually any game” carries one honest asterisk: a few competitive titles demand kernel-level anti-cheat that won’t run here. If your main game is one of them, this isn’t your machine yet.

  • People who want the OS to decide quietly for them

    Updates at 3 AM, telemetry by default, settings that move while you’re not looking. Vizsla asks, waits, and explains — if you’d rather not be asked, you’ll find it chatty.

  • People shopping for a locked-down appliance

    A box that does five things and refuses the sixth. Vizsla is the opposite of an appliance: it does nearly everything, and it lets you rearrange all of it.

Meet the Vizsla

Every operating system has a face. This one has a dog. The vizsla is all over Vizsla OS — in the wallpapers your day opens on, in the portraits and the search hound. These pictures are the system’s own artwork, the very images that ship in it — no stock photos, and no photographs at all: the Vizsla of Vizsla is drawn, not photographed. The wallpaper shelf is kept short on purpose: five, each one earning its place.

What makes it different

Nothing requires an account

Apps may offer a sign-in. The system never demands one. Your files live on your machines, and a “no” from you is recorded and never re-asked.

Updates that wait for you

Updates never lock you out of your own machine mid-work. They wait until you’re away, the machine is plugged in, and the network is unmetered — and they tell you what they’re waiting for, in plain words.

One system, every shape

Desktop, tablet, TV, console, phone, dash — the same apps and files, rearranged for the screen they’re on, never reduced. When the hardware is ready, we are ready.

A fleet that installs itself

Software is built once and lands, verified hash-by-hash, on every computer in the house that can run it. Drift is noticed, not discovered by accident.

One calm inbox for decisions

Every decision the system needs from its human collects in one place — Pass or Paws — to be answered in one sitting, in plain words. No app ambushes you with its own pop-up.

The Baking Hour

A new Vizsla machine sits and gestates for at least an hour after install — indexing, warming, pre-fetching — so it is fabulous the first time you touch it, not the fiftieth.

Preloaded first — and a magazine if you wander

Every version of Vizsla OS arrives with all the apps most people will ever want or need already aboard — so the store had the freedom to be something better than a store. App stores elsewhere are all the same: paid promotions, unrelated ads, millions of crappy apps, clutter. Vizsla’s is the Shelf: a digital magazine that focuses on a handful of apps at a time, in a visual, narrative layout like a paper magazine.

It’s calm. It asks for nothing. You can completely ignore it or linger on the pages — and the back issues are always there when you want them. Meet the Shelf.

And you can cancel that subscription

Vizsla’s apps quietly stand in for a stack of paid ones — the grammar subscription, the office suite, the PDF plan, the read-it-later app, the password vault, the backup plan, the home-upkeep tracker. Priced from the vendors’ own pages on 27 October 2026, the everyday home stack comes to $885.51 a year you stop paying — and the business shelf (menu boards, kitchen software, safety logs, the business books) adds $1,560 a year more. See the honest arithmetic — every price sourced, every “covers the everyday job, not the whole suite” said out loud.

And for the subscriptions you already have: Money Leaks, the audit inside the books, shows you the ones you’ve stopped using and warns you before one renews — and if a vendor insists on a letter, it drafts the cancellation letter for you. The cancelling itself is always your tap. Vizsla never cancels anything behind your back.

Kind, in the small mechanics

If an app disappears and the system doesn’t know why, it asks — once, gently:

“hi it’s me. Sorry to bother but I’m not sure if you want to uninstall this. Please let me know if you want it reinstalled or not. No worries either way.”

If you removed it on purpose, it never asks at all. That is the design philosophy in one paragraph: operating systems have competed on power and polish for forty years. Nobody competed on being kind to the person at the keyboard. More on kindness & privacy.
