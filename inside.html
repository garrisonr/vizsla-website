What’s inside

Every app below exists and is installed on the machines that run Vizsla today — names and versions taken from the same Family Catalog the fleet installs from (generation 703, read 13 November 2026). Things still being built are not listed as done. That’s the rule for this whole site.

Try one piece today: System Profiler

Vizsla OS itself has no download yet — but this app does. Linux System Profiler is Vizsla’s own machine profiler and updates hub — think Apple’s System Information, for Linux, plus the thing Apple never gave it: one place to see driver, firmware, and software updates, and apply them only when you say so, group by group. It also stands alone, free, on any Linux PC.

The public test build is v0.8.8 — a single binary for Linux (x86_64, about 22 MB). Download it, make it executable (chmod +x), and run it. No installer, no account. The honest limits, in its own words: it is early development with rough edges, tested primarily on Ubuntu-family systems, and a few steps behind the 0.9.3 build that ships inside Vizsla OS. Bug reports genuinely shape the next release.

AudioWaves 1.54.19

The music player. Your files, played as they are — and when something in the path changes them, AudioWaves says so, in plain words, under the track that is playing.

FLAC, kept whole

FLAC files play losslessly, including 24-bit and high-sample-rate recordings. The line under the track names what is playing — for example, “FLAC 24/96” — from the file itself, not from its name.

DSD, sent as DSD

DSF and DFF files play through AudioWaves’ own DSD reader. The DSD bits ride to a DAC that accepts them as DoP — DSD over PCM frames at 176.4 kHz — untouched by volume, effects, or resampling, and the playing line says “DSD via DoP” while it happens.

Your DAC, picked by name

Settings has an output picker that lists the devices your machine can actually play through — a USB DAC among them — with the rates each one owns. Two ways to play: Follow the music, which asks the shared sound system to run the device at each song’s own rate, or DAC Direct, which gives the device to AudioWaves alone and applies no digital volume at all.

Bit-perfect, when it is

Bit-perfect mode stands the extras down — no equalizer, no ReplayGain, no resampling. AudioWaves tries the recording’s own sample rate first, and only prints “bit-perfect” while the path really is unchanged. If a device tops out below a track’s rate, the track is resampled to the best rate that device has, and the Signal Path line names the change instead of hiding it.

A signal path you can check

Every playing track carries a Signal Path: the source, each step the sound takes, and the device at the end, proven by reading the device back — its real rate and format — not by trusting what was asked for. When a read-back can’t be had, the line says the device state is unverified rather than guessing.

Music in more than one room

The Rooms room plays the house together. AudioWaves can send what it is playing to other rooms, or join another source as a room itself, in sync — timing is measured, and the room says how close it is. It speaks Sendspin, an open whole-home protocol, so rooms are not limited to copies of AudioWaves, and it also runs the family’s own whole-home groups, where a box joins only when someone at that box says yes.

Playlists, yours and brought-in

Make playlists by hand, save what is playing as one, or let a smart playlist keep itself — Recently Added, Never Played, and Back Again arrive already made. Playlists you already have come in too: M3U, M3U8, and XSPF files import, and the importer tells you how many entries matched music in your library. Playlists live in AudioWaves’ own library on your machine.

Playlists that live in more than one place

If your playlists are split across services — some in Apple Music, some in Spotify, Tidal, Napster or SoundCloud — we recommend TuneMyMusic. It copies playlists from one service to another and, on its paid plan, keeps them in sync: change a playlist in one place and the copy follows. It is a website, not another app to install.

To be plain about it: TuneMyMusic is not ours. Vizsla does not make it, does not sell it, and gets nothing if you sign up. We recommend it because it does this one job well, and because it does not hold your playlists hostage — it can export a playlist as a file (M3U, CSV, TXT or XML) that stays on your own machine. A file like that is one honest way to bring a playlist into a local player such as Audio Waves, instead of leaving it locked inside a streaming service.

The honest price, checked November 2026: there is a free tier, capped at 500 tracks, with export to a text or CSV file. Syncing needs the Premium plan — $24 a year, or $5.50 a month, cancel anytime. Premium removes the track cap and allows up to 20 automatic syncs. Prices change, so check the live figure before you pay: TuneMyMusic plans · How its transfers work

Bringing a playlist home, exactly: in TuneMyMusic, export the playlist as an M3U file (a Premium export — the free tier’s text and CSV files are not ones AudioWaves opens). In AudioWaves, import that file: M3U, M3U8 and XSPF all come in, matched against the music already in your library, and the importer tells you plainly how many entries matched and how many did not. A streaming playlist names songs; your library has to actually hold them — what it holds, it queues up as a playlist of your own.

Film Ferret 0.1.0

The film library. Film Ferret keeps the catalog of your films — the titles, posters, years, and facts — and leaves the films themselves exactly where they already live: on your own drives, in the collection you built.

That deserves saying plainly, because it is the whole design: no torrents and no copyrighted media are included — not in Film Ferret, and not in Vizsla OS. The catalog holds filenames and metadata, and a filename is enough: enough to shelve a film, search for it, and draw a century of cinema up by decade — without a single film ever being inside the app.

A catalog of names, not films

Every entry is a title and its facts — its year, its poster, who made it, what it is. Nothing plays from the catalog, because nothing is in it. It is the card index, not the vault.

Your collection, organized

These are simply filenames, kept so you can organize the collection you already have — by decade, by director, by the hundred questions a real collection answers. What you own stops being a pile of files and becomes a library you can browse.

Playlists you can share

Build a playlist — a director’s whole run, a festival’s program, a decade in twenty films — and share it with other Vizsla OS users. What travels is the list: again, just the film titles and metadata, not the files themselves. On their machine, their Ferret matches the titles against their own collection.

And one special edition — WordCraft Plus

WordCraft appears in this box twice, on purpose. The citizen above is the finished app as its makers ship it. WordCraft Plus is the family’s own edition of it: it sits in the family catalog above, and it updates through Vizsla’s catalog like every family app, so upstream WordCraft releases don’t apply to it.

What Plus adds is a writing desk the base version doesn’t have. Writing is organized as scenes — one scene model — with a binder to hold them and a corkboard to arrange them. A typewriter focus keeps your place as you write, including a forward-only mode for drafts that shouldn’t be fiddled with. A lens watches for echoes — words and phrases you have already used — and spellcheck runs live, with a running word count in the status bar.
