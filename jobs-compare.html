Business tools

IDenty Pro vs the Job Boards

Every job board on this page sells the same thing under different paint: access to your own applicants. The employer pays per post, per slot, per click, or per “sponsored” boost in an auction against other employers. The application is born inside the platform’s database, the candidate relationship belongs to the platform, and the platform rents it back — resume-database access, messaging credits, per-applicant fees. A posting deleted in one silo lives on in the others. And the ten names that started this page — LinkedIn, ZipRecruiter, Glassdoor, Monster, CareerBuilder, SimplyHired, Dice, Wellfound, Snagajob and Handshake — turn out to be about six independent sellers in 2026, once you add the seven columns an honest chart cannot omit: Indeed (the largest board in the country, and the posting engine behind two of the ten), USAJobs, Google for Jobs, FlexJobs, We Work Remotely, Built In and Idealist.

IDenty Pro’s answer is structural, not a discount. The posting belongs to the institution. It is published on the institution’s own board, on its own instance, at $0 — the last step of a governed requisition, not a product in a store. The application is born in the same record that will run the employment. And with §88 — the jobs federation, specced and queued as this page is written — independent IDenty instances will repost each other’s jobs directly, peer to peer, under each receiving admin’s own policy rules. No platform in the middle, because there is nothing in the middle to charge rent.

How to read the marks. For the seventeen: ✓ means the capability is a standard part of the product; Partial means it exists but is shallow, capped, or partner-delivered; — means it isn’t offered; Add-on means the capability is real but sold as a separately priced extra on top of the product you already bought. For IDenty Pro the column uses words — Yes, Partial, No — with the specification section (§) that backs each grade. Planned means exactly that: specced, not built — and it is graded No until it ships. Prices are labelled published (on the vendor’s own page), quote-only, or market data (buyer and procurement reporting — Vendr, SEC filings, pricing guides), and were read in November 2026. Vendors reprice without notice; so do we not — $0 has been stable for some time.

The toll — what one hire actually costs

18 prices

Before the feature chart, the money — because the money is the complaint. Five facts carry the section. LinkedIn charges more per seat when you add seats ($170/mo for one Recruiter Lite licence; $270/mo each for seats 2–5, on its own table). Indeed’s “free” posting is a funnel into an auction whose effective price is $15–$50 per application. ZipRecruiter’s own SEC filing prices the average toll at ≈$556/mo per paying employer. Glassdoor charges employers to remove competitors’ ads from their own company page. And Monster+ Pro’s $299/mo is a credit pool that burns on both ends — the same expiring credits pay for clicks and for resume views at $2.00 a peek. The full table:

The honest concessions

6 of them

This page’s credibility is its only asset, so the moats get printed at the same size as the tolls.

1. LinkedIn’s graph is a real moat

A billion-member professional graph, twenty years deep, cannot be declared into existence. IDenty’s answer is different in kind, not degree: the lifelong Person record means an institution’s graph is grown, not rented — every applicant, employee, student, patient and alumnus the institution has ever legitimately touched is already a node, with provenance. The chart grades networking Partial, and here is the sentence in full size: we will never have LinkedIn’s graph; we make yours unnecessary to rent for the people you actually hire.

2. Indeed’s traffic is a real moat

No federation of independent instances launches with Indeed’s seeker volume. IDenty’s answers: (a) Google for Jobs — free, structured-data distribution off the institution’s own board, the one top-of-funnel that cannot be toll-gated; (b) federation reach grows with instances, and the honest early claim is your region, your sector, your alumni — not “everyone.” Seeker-traffic rows stay Partial.

3. Glassdoor’s review corpus is a real moat — and a hostage situation

The reviews are valuable and the reviewed employer pays to remove competitors’ ads from its own page. IDenty has no public review corpus and grades No — while noting its answer is structural: inside IDenty, reputation data (360° feedback §64, shout-outs §69) belongs to the institution and the person, not to a third party renting it back. A public, person-controlled review layer is a possible future item; it is not shipped and is not graded as planned here.

4. Handshake’s school network is a real moat

1,400+ institutional partnerships is a genuine network. IDenty’s counter is that its institutions are schools: a district or university running IDenty already holds its own students’ records from kindergarten to retirement, so its “campus network” is its own enrollment, not a rented channel. Grade: Partial; the cross-institution campus reach is federation-dependent.

5. What IDenty genuinely wins today, no asterisk

The toll rows ($0 posting, $0 candidate side, no per-view credits, no seat licences), the application-becomes-the-record row — no board can match it, because their product ends at the application — the built-in ATS row, and, once §88 ships, the federation and employer-policy rows, which no column in this table offers at all: every Partial there is a platform curating for itself, never an employer enforcing its own rules (no commission-only, no categories it won’t carry, nothing beyond its chosen radius) on jobs it accepts from peers.

6. The federation’s cold start is the honest risk

A peer-to-peer board is worth little with two instances and a great deal with two hundred. The mechanism ships complete and the network is the work — the same sentence every honest network product owes its readers, and the one none of the incumbents ever had to print, because they bought their networks with the tolls documented above.

How the federation works — §88

specced, not yet built
Status: specified, not yet built. §88’s specification (SPEC-JOBS-FEDERATION) is written — the mechanism below is settled, with 14 E2E acceptance criteria — and its build is queued in the benchmark loop behind the current lane. Nothing in this section is graded as shipped anywhere on this page; the chart above marks both §88 rows No until the build lands and passes its audits.

No platform in the middle

An institution publishes its own jobs on its own careers page — the posting is a structured document (category, compensation structure, work mode, postal code: data, not prose) signed with the institution’s own Ed25519 key, the same machinery that signs facts on the record. Instances then share those documents directly. There is no registry, no central directory, no relay — and therefore nothing in the middle that can charge rent, run an auction, or hold an application hostage. Funding for the whole mechanism: $0 to start, $0 monthly, $0 annual — transport is HTTPS between instances that already serve it, and the ZIP-code centroids come from the U.S. Census Bureau’s public-domain Gazetteer files. The one thing that could ever cost money — paying for reach — is refused on principle: the design contains no sponsored slot and no place to put one.

Pairing — deliberate, mutual, revocable

Two instances connect the way two institutions do: on purpose. An admin creates a one-time invitation (72-hour life, shown as text and a QR); the other admin presents it, and both sides approve — with both instances’ key fingerprints displayed for comparison over a channel the admins already trust. Nothing flows before both approvals exist. Either side can unpair at any moment, effective immediately, or block a fingerprint outright. No public surface lists an instance’s peers, and a pairing endpoint probed by a stranger answers with silence shaped like a 404. Key rotation means re-pairing: trust is never silently inherited.

The policy engine — the receiving admin’s law

Origin asserts; receiver disposes. No peer can force a posting onto your board, keep it there against your policy, or resurrect it after you pull it. The rules are written as plain sentences, never as code an admin must learn to read — and the owner’s three examples are the Owner’s starter policy preset, verbatim in effect:

  • “No exotic dancers” — a category rule. The category vocabulary names adult entertainment precisely so an admin can refuse it; a platform that cannot name a category cannot let you block it. In the starter policy this block is hard: it has no release valve, and the only way past it is editing the policy itself, in the open, with the diff logged.
  • “No commission only” — a compensation-structure rule. Every posting must declare its comp structure from a closed list (salary, hourly, salary plus commission, commission only, stipend, per assignment, unpaid, undisclosed) before it can publish or travel. The starter policy refuses commission only and undisclosed — “no comp stated” is a refusal too.
  • “No companies further than 100 miles from our corporate home ZIP” — a distance rule: the posting’s ZIP centroid, measured from the institution’s home ZIP centroid, must fall within the radius the admin sets. Remote postings are exempt by default; hybrid postings are not — a hybrid job 250 miles away is a commute lie, and the rule says so.

Around those: keyword rules (matched on word boundaries, with the matched term shown in its sentence — a zoo’s “exotic animal keeper” is the standing warning), employer deny/allow lists, age caps that kill evergreen zombie reposts, and duplicate collapse, so cross-posting the same job through five paired instances buys no extra board space. A peer’s own narrowing can only ever narrow what you accept, never widen it. And the default for a fresh institution is review everything: every incoming posting lands in a queue until the admin writes rules from their own decisions. A refusal always names the rule that fired and shows its evidence; a soft refusal can be released once, by a named admin, with a reason recorded forever. No silent drops, no silent publishes.

A repost is a copy that can die

A repost renders from the signed document stored locally — never hot-linked, never fetched at display time — and four executioners can end it, any one sufficient: a superseding version arrives (and re-runs policy, so an origin cannot edit its way around a block); a takedown document arrives (filled, closed, or withdrawn at the origin); the signed expiry passes — death that needs no network at all, so a posting dies on schedule even if its origin has been offline for a week; or the receiving admin pulls it, instantly, for any reason or none, and a locally pulled repost stays pulled. The staleness bound is printed on the tin: a takedown lands at the earlier of the push (attempted within minutes) or the next signed-manifest reconciliation — worst case the configured interval, 24 hours by default, settable to one. A removed repost’s URL serves an honest tombstone — “This posting was shared from {institution} and is no longer available” — never a silent 404 and never a still-apply-able ghost. Five bad signatures inside an hour auto-pause a pairing and alert the admins: a spoofing attempt becomes noise and a phone call, not a board full of fake jobs.

Applications go home

A repost is a link, never a form. Applying happens entirely at the origin institution, in its own applicant tracking, through one of two doors: a record-holder points their lifelong record at the institution (the shipped pattern), or a stranger applies on the origin’s careers page and a Person record is born there — applicant only, with a consent fact and provenance, deduplicated by identity (the phone number is an identity) rather than by hope. The reposting instance serves the link and nothing else: no applicant data transits a reposter — not the application, not a copy, not even a count. Bulletin boards don’t take applications; that is the whole design. And the public careers page emits schema.org JobPosting structured data alongside the HTML, so Google for Jobs carries the posting free — chronological order, no ranking knob, because there is no ranking knob to sell.
