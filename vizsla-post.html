For Businesses · Newsletters

Vizsla Post

Our own newsletter tool — written in Rust, running on our own server, sending your words to your list without handing the list to anyone. You write an issue, it goes to your subscribers, and it publishes to a public archive on the web. The beehiiv-and-Substack job, with no landlord.

Seated now on our own server · the Vizsla OS list is opening · no issue has been sent yet

Why we built it

Every newsletter platform rents you your own audience. The list you spent years earning sits in somebody else’s database, the rent rises with every subscriber, and leaving means an export file and a prayer. Vizsla Post is the opposite shape: one small program, on a server in Leesburg, Florida, holding the list in a single database file its owner can copy with one command. When the tool is boring, the writing gets to be interesting.

The Vizsla OS list

News, when there’s news.

Release notes, new tools, and the occasional letter from the workshop. Join below and we’ll send a confirmation email — click its link and you’re on the list. That double opt-in is deliberate: nobody lands on this list by accident, and nobody is added by anybody else.

  • No spam, ever. News only when there’s news.
  • Unsubscribe in one click, anytime — in every email, effective immediately, no hard feelings.
  • Your address is never sold or shared. Read the privacy policy — it’s short, and it means what it says.

What it does

Write, review, send

A calm editor with autosaved drafts, a pre-send review that checks the subject, the unsubscribe link, the sender, and the recipient count before anything leaves — and sends you can schedule, test-send to yourself, and resume if the machine blinks. A crashed send resumes; it never restarts and double-mails your list.

A list kept honest

Double opt-in by default, a welcome email on confirmation, and suppression discipline underneath it all: bounces, complaints, and unsubscribes are kept out of future sends and imports automatically, and an address comes back only with fresh consent.

Imports without the junk

Bring a list from beehiiv, Substack, Mailchimp, or a plain CSV. The importer drops invalid addresses, typo domains (gmial.com), disposable inboxes, and duplicates — and reports exactly who was left out, and why.

An archive on the web

Every published issue is also a web page: a public archive with an index, per-issue pages, and the signup form built in. Read the Vizsla OS archive — it fills as issues publish.

Safety rails

New lists send in steps, gated on bounce and complaint rates, so a fresh sender builds a reputation instead of burning one. Past the safety thresholds a send pauses itself and says so in plain words. The analytics count real readers — link-scanner traffic is filtered out before it can flatter you.

Driven by people — and agents

Everything in the dashboard is reachable through a REST API and an MCP server, with the send tools marked destructive so an agent asks before it mails your list. Sign-in is an email allowlist with a code flow: no passwords to leak, and only listed people get in.

How it compares

Every newsletter platform publishes a comparison chart, and every chart is a highlight reel. This one follows the rule of the IDenty Pro chart: the misses are printed in the same size type as the wins. The field here is the hosted set a small list actually chooses among — Mailchimp, beehiiv, Kit, Buttondown and MailerLite — priced at the scale that matters to a working list: a few hundred subscribers, growing to a few thousand.

How to read the marks. For the five hosted tools: ✓ means the capability is a standard part of the product; Partial means it exists but is shallow or gated behind a higher plan; — means it isn’t offered; Add-on means it is sold as a separately priced bundle on top of the plan. For Vizsla Post the column uses words — Yes, Partial, Missing — because a young product owes you the sentence, not just the symbol. A ? means the vendor doesn’t publish enough for us to mark the cell honestly — we’d rather print the question mark than guess. Prices are the vendors’ published US list prices, read on November 27, 2026.

Three cells deserve their footnotes in the open. What the bill counts is the quiet one: Mailchimp bills for every stored contact — subscribed, unsubscribed and non-subscribed alike — and the same person in two audiences counts twice; only archived or deleted contacts stop counting. It is the single most repeated complaint in its reviews, and it is a category of bill Vizsla Post cannot send, because there is no bill. Buttondown’s headline price is its base: tagging, automations and more are priced bundles on top (at 1,000 subscribers the full-featured price is $29 a month), and its send limit cell is its own pricing assumption — at most one email a day to the full list. And the $0 rows for beehiiv and Kit are real free plans, generously capped — with the automation paywall standing exactly where our own Missing stands: beehiiv’s free plan has no automations at all, and neither does Kit’s.

What a chart can’t show: the safety rails. Vizsla Post sends a new list in steps, pauses itself past 2.5% hard bounces or 0.08% complaints, checks its suppression list before every send and every import, cleans imports and reports exactly who was left out and why, and resumes a crashed send instead of restarting it. The hosted five manage reputation invisibly, inside their own walls; the self-hosted tools that match our data ownership — listmonk, Mautic, phpList — leave deliverability entirely to you. This is the only self-hosted tool in the survey that refuses to let a fresh sender hurt himself.

A last honesty note on the prices themselves: this category reprices constantly. beehiiv raised its prices in October 2026, MailerLite renamed and repriced its plans in June 2026, and Mailchimp cut its free tier in half in February 2026. Every number above was true when read; none of them is ours to keep true. Vizsla Post’s price is the one cell in the chart that cannot change, because there isn’t one.

Four ways to send

Sending is a plug, not a marriage. Vizsla Post speaks to four kinds of sending service behind one shape, and switching is a settings change, not a migration:

  • Microsoft 365 — a named preset that sends as your own business mailbox. The Vizsla OS list sends this way: zero added cost, sized for real lists.
  • Amazon SES — the bulk truck, at roughly a dime per thousand emails, when a list outgrows a mailbox.
  • PostShiba — a sending service with a slot built to its documented shape, ready the day an invitation lands.
  • Your own mail server — local SMTP, or any generic SMTP relay, for organizations that run their own post office.

Sending ships switched off. It turns on when a provider is configured and its domain checks — DKIM, SPF, DMARC, each verified live by the app — pass.

The privacy posture

The list is the readers’ property on loan. Addresses are never sold, rented, or shared; the data lives on our own server, not in a marketing cloud; every email carries a one-click unsubscribe that takes effect immediately and never asks you to sign in to leave. The full policy — what’s collected, what it’s for, how to be deleted — is on the privacy page, in plain language.

The honest state

Vizsla Post is seated on our own server today and its first list — the Vizsla OS list, the form above — is opening. No issue has been sent yet; the archive is empty because there is nothing in it yet, and this page will not pretend otherwise. It was built first for our own list, the way every tool on this shelf starts. Organizations curious about running it are welcome to write: admin@vizslaos.com.

Vizsla Post is a clean-room Rust reimplementation inspired by Mailcheap, an open-source newsletter app by Marc Lou (AGPL-3.0). No upstream code ships in Vizsla Post; the credit is for the feature blueprint, and for proving the shape works. Vizsla Post itself is AGPL-3.0 software.
