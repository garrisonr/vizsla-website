Get involved

Help it grow

This is truly a labor of love, and it got built the way it will keep getting better: out loud, on the record, one decision at a time. If something is clumsy, say so. If something is missing, name it. And if you’d like to do more than say so, this page is the honest menu.

No corporate sponsors — on purpose

Unlike a lot of Linux distributions, Vizsla OS has no corporate sponsorships. That isn’t because the cash wouldn’t be welcome — it would. It’s because sponsorship money likes to arrive with opinions, and editorial input is a hard no. Nobody buys a say in what this system becomes, what it ships, or what it refuses to do. It stays a labor of love, which means it runs on the people who believe in it. There are four ways to be one of those people:

1. Be a beta tester

The honest state first: Vizsla OS is in active development, and there is no public download of the OS yet. Early testers will be the first outside hands on it — people willing to run unfinished software, say plainly what breaks, and be kind about the rough edges while they’re being sanded.

To put your name down, open an issue on the project’s GitHub and say hello: github.com/garrisonr/vizsla-website/issues. Tell us what machines you’ve got and what you’d put it through. When builds are ready for outside hands, testers come from that list, in order.

Want something in your hands today? System Profiler — one of Vizsla’s own apps — is already a public download, and its bug reports shape the OS too.

2. Donate

Vizsla is free, and free is the point — but the work runs on real things: machines to build and test on, electricity, domains, and the AI tokens that power the development itself. A donation doesn’t buy features, votes, or a logo anywhere. It just keeps the lights on for a project that answers to its users instead of its sponsors.

One of those costs has a name and a toll booth: making Gmail work inside Vizsla’s own mail app means passing Google’s verification, and Google’s verification requires a CASA security assessment — roughly $540 to $1,800 a year, every year. That’s the price of playing in Google’s yard, and donations are how a project with no sponsors pays it.

Curio — the collection app being built for Vizsla — has two toll booths of its own. Today Curio does the job the free way: you add pieces by hand, use your own photos, and follow a link out when you want to check a value. Nothing is locked, and nothing is held back. But two integrations would make it considerably nicer, and both of them charge for the door:

  • hobbyDB — the catalog behind Pop Price Guide; its data powers the values in the Funko app itself. With its API, Curio could match your Funko Pops and action figures against the real catalog — scan a barcode or search a name instead of typing every figure in by hand — and show hobbyDB’s estimated values, built from millions of price points and verified eBay sales, right on your own shelves. The price of that door: a one-off set-up fee of at least $1,200, plus monthly charges on top — hobbyDB doesn’t publish the monthly amount; it’s set per project.
  • PriceCharting — for video games. Its API would let Curio show your games’ current values inside your own copy of the app — prices drawn from real sold listings, by condition: loose, complete in box, new, graded — refreshing as the market moves, instead of a value you pinned once by hand and a link you follow out. The price: $49 a month ($588 a year), on the only plan their API comes with.

If donations ever covered those, that is exactly what they would buy — a convenience, not a ransom. Curio works without them, and it always will. But if you’d like your Pops to import themselves and your games to price themselves, this is what stands between here and there, priced in the open.

And then there are the doors money alone can’t open — closed or gated, each in its own way:

  • eBay Marketplace Insights — what things actually sold for is the ground truth for figures, Pops, physical media, sneakers, and books, and today Curio sends you out to eBay to see it. eBay classes its pricing data as a restricted API, specially granted to select developers. There is no price published and no price to pay — only an application to file. If it ever came through, the real sold prices would live on the shelf beside the item instead of a tap away.
  • StockX — the sneaker market’s big board. Its API is for approved developers only, mostly sellers tending their own listings, and there is no published price for getting in. If a door ever opened to a reader like Curio, it is how live sneaker prices would reach your shelves.
  • Packet — the paid successor to KicksDB, which has stopped taking new sign-ups. It resells sneaker data gathered from the big marketplaces. Its Developer tier runs about $32 a month, with a free tier underneath it, and it would give the kicks shelf a real catalog to look things up in — today sneakers are Curio’s weakest shelf, a ledger of style codes kept by hand.
  • UPCitemdb — the barcode phone book. Its free tier already answers 100 look-ups a day, and Curio uses look-ups like that to turn a barcode into a title. Paid tiers lift the ceiling — and a higher ceiling is the difference between scanning a whole shelf of boxes in an afternoon and feeding them in a hundred at a time, for everything that carries a UPC.

Some of these may never open to a project this small — approvals aren’t for sale, and no donation changes that. They’re on the page anyway: an honest map, locked rooms and all, beats a cheerful one that pretends they aren’t there.

There is a longer list, and it needs one thing said plainly before any of it: the capabilities in the list below are not here today — not one of them is in Vizsla OS yet. They are expansions we would build only if two things were both true: people actually want them, so the demand is there, and the funding is provided to cover what they cost. Until both are true, Vizsla does these same jobs the free way, the way it does today — and nothing below is a promise, a plan with a date, or a feature to wait for. It is a menu of what community money could buy, priced in the open, and it falls into three parts: costs a community could cover outright, costs that are each business’s own rent no matter what, and doors money can’t open at all.

What community money could cover outright. These are fixed, published numbers — the kind a community can cover together, after which the capability would simply be included for everyone:

AudioWaves — podcasts

  • Taddy Pro — $75 a month. A podcast data service with 100,000 requests and transcripts included — and, rare and precious for an operating system built to work offline, a licence that expressly allows caching the data. AudioWaves could search and describe podcasts from a real well instead of scraping.
  • Listen Notes Pro — $200 a month in any month it is used, and nothing in a month it isn’t; 5,000 requests included, then $1.60 for each extra thousand. Another podcast well: search, metadata, transcripts, and listen scores.

Reader — the book shelves

  • ISBNdb Premium — $35.99 a month ($359 a year). Complete book metadata by ISBN at real scale — 15,000 look-ups a day — beyond what the free sources cover.

Weather & maps

  • WeatherAPI.com Pro+ — $25 a month. Radar, satellite, and lightning layers, and weather along a route.
  • MapTiler Cloud Flex — $30 a month. Commercial map tiles beyond the free quota.
  • OpenWeather — the first 1,000 calls a day are free; beyond that, about a sixth of a cent per call, or about $40 a month for its Startup tier. Weather data at scale, priced by the sip.

Sniff Search

  • Brave Search API — $5 per 1,000 requests, with a $5 credit every month. An independent search index behind Sniff Search — with the catch stated plainly: Brave’s terms allow no caching beyond transient use and no derivative works, so it would answer questions, not build a library.

Gaming

  • RAWG Business — $149 a month. A database of half a million games, licensed for commercial use, behind the Arcade’s catalog — with no redistribution: the data would live inside Vizsla’s own apps and nowhere else.

Small business

  • Xero Core — about $24 a month. The app-partner tier that would let a Vizsla business tool sync invoices and books with a shop’s real Xero accounts.
  • DocuSign Developer Starter — $600 a year ($50 a month, billed by the year). Legally valid contract signing without leaving the app.
  • Dropbox Sign API Essentials — from $75 a month. The same trick by another door: signature requests sent, signed, and filed inside the tool.

Building trades

  • ICC Digital Codes Premium Complete — $1,056 a year. One shared licence would put the full building, electrical, and plumbing code library behind a job-site code lookup for every trades user — the cleanest single target on this whole page.
  • Craftsman National Estimator Cloud — $167.88 a year. All ten Craftsman cost books behind a small-trades estimator.

Clinics

  • pVerify — from $125 a month. Real-time insurance eligibility checks a small clinic could run from its front-desk app, instead of a dozen payer portals.

Real estate

  • SimplyRETS — $49 a month per MLS feed, plus a $99 one-time connection. One feed would give an independent agent live listings without building the plumbing from scratch.

Legal

  • PACER — $0.10 a page, capped at $3 a document, and free in any quarter that stays under $30. Federal court records for a case tracker — a modest donation pool would cover a small firm’s docket pulls for a year.

Receipts

  • Veryfi — $0.08 per receipt and $0.16 per invoice, turned into structured expense data a business tool can actually use.

Farms — the honest freebies

  • John Deere Operations Center and Granular Insights — genuinely $0. No donation needed and none asked: both platforms are free, and the work there is ours — building the offline, mixed-fleet field notebook that sits on top of them.

Each business’s own rent. The next list is different, and the difference matters: these are per-seat and per-transaction costs that scale with the business itself. Donations cannot cover them — nobody’s gift pays another shop’s card fees — and they are listed so the numbers sit in the open. What donations could fund here is the plumbing: building the integrations once, so a business plugs in its own account and the tool just works.

Card processing

  • Stripe — 2.9% plus 30 cents of every online sale. Paid out of each sale, by the seller — there is nothing for a donation to cover.
  • Square — 2.6% plus 15 cents per in-person sale, on the same terms: the business’s own cost of taking cards.

Field-service software

  • ServiceTitan — $199 to $299 per technician, per month. A five-tech shop pays a thousand dollars a month and up before it has turned a wrench.
  • Jobber — from $29 a month.
  • Housecall Pro — from $59 a month.

Auto repair data

  • ALLDATA Repair — $209 a month. The factory procedures, torque specs, and wiring diagrams at the bay.
  • Identifix Direct-Hit — from $209 a month. Symptom-and-code searches against millions of confirmed fixes.
  • PartsTech — from $45 a month. Live parts lookup and ordering inside the repair order — the cheapest door in its family.

Trucking

  • DAT load boards — $59 to $339 a month per carrier. The loads and lane rates owner-operators live by.

Estimating data

  • RSMeans Data Online — from $408 a year. Current unit costs for pricing construction work — the pro tier above the Craftsman books.

Healthcare coding

  • The AMA’s CPT licence — $1,050 a year, plus $18.50 per user per year. Any app that shows CPT billing codes owes the American Medical Association this toll; there is no free edition and no way around it.

Legal research

  • UniCourt — from $59 a month. State and federal case search in one place.
  • Westlaw Edge — from $155.35 a month. The research library itself — at this tier a link out, not something an app can embed.

Restaurants

  • DoorDash — 15%, 25%, or 30% of every delivery order, and 6% on pickup. Not rent exactly — a bite of every sale.
  • OpenTable — $149 to $499 a month, plus $1 to $1.50 for every diner its network seats.
  • Toast POS — from $69 a month per location.

Retail

  • Lightspeed Retail — from $89 a month per outlet.

Salons

  • Vagaro — about $24 to $30 a month per calendar.
  • Booksy — $29.99 a month.

Insurance offices

  • HawkSoft — $99 per concurrent user, per month. The management system small agencies run on.

Clinic front desk

  • Availity Essentials Plus — $25 a month. One login for eligibility and claims across payers.

Hiring and screening deserves its own honest paragraph, because it is the one place the law draws the line. Checkr — the outlier in its industry — publishes its prices: background checks from $29.99 a report, drug screening from $10 a test. But under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, screening runs under each employer’s own credentialed account: the employer signs the agreement, certifies the reason, and gets the candidate’s consent. A community cannot buy background checks for businesses, and Vizsla cannot hold one master account and resell them. What donations could fund is the connector — the one-click pipe, built once, that lets a shop plug in its own Checkr, Accurate Background, or eScreen account and order from inside its onboarding tool, instead of drowning in portals and paperwork.

Agriculture

  • Climate FieldView — from about $350 a year per farm. Planting and harvest maps, pulled into an offline field notebook.

And one that isn’t a door at all: TuneMyMusic is a consumer subscription for moving your own playlists between services — there is no developer API behind it for a project to fund, and AudioWaves already imports playlist files on its own, free.

More doors money can’t open. The four above aren’t the only ones. These are gated the same way — no published price and no price to pay, only applications, contracts, and certifications:

  • hobbyDB’s monthly charge — even the door above has a second lock: the set-up fee is printed, but the monthly amount is set per project and never published.
  • Antstream’s catalogue — the licensed retro-games library. No partner or API programme exists at any price, and Antstream’s own subscription, from $3.99 a month, licenses one person’s account — not an operating system. Licensed retro games can’t be bundled into Vizsla by subscription; only a negotiated platform deal could do that, and those aren’t on a menu.
  • Plaid, Akoya, and Finicity — bank and account data. Usage-priced, production access by application, and no public price list from any of them.
  • Licensed lyrics — Musixmatch and LyricFind hold the rights to show song words legally, and neither publishes a price.
  • ACRCloud — music recognition — name that tune, at scale — priced by quote only.
  • TheTVDB — television metadata and artwork, available to projects only under a negotiated licence.
  • Tomorrow.io — weather data beyond its free tier, quote only.
  • Surescripts and DrFirst — e-prescribing, reachable only through certified partners. There is no price at any number, because the door is a certification, not a checkout.
  • Accurate Background, eScreen, and Sterling — screening again: contracts under each employer’s own account, as the law requires — which is why the connector above is the fundable part.
  • Procore — construction management, priced by a firm’s construction volume and never published.
  • Mitchell 1 — auto repair information, quote only.
  • Motive and Samsara — fleet telematics and dashcams, quote only, per fleet.
  • MLS access generally — the listings themselves. Stellar MLS, the multiple listing service for the Leesburg area, charges vendors $7,500 a year per product just to be allowed to show its data; the SimplyRETS feed above is a bridge across that wall, not a hole in it.
  • Brightwheel and Procare — childcare management, quote only.
  • Passare — funeral case management, quote only.
  • Cloudbeds and Mews — the systems small hotels run on, quote only.
  • EZLynx and Applied Epic — insurance agency platforms, quote only.
  • Intuit’s QuickBooks developer terms — unpublished; whether Intuit charges apps to integrate isn’t something it prints.

One last honesty note, on memberships: some doors are per-person by design. Simkl PRO ($34.99 a year), Trakt VIP, and fanart.tv VIP are memberships in one person’s own tracking account — the project can’t buy them for its users. And some things need no funding at all: OMDb’s free tier already allows 1,000 look-ups a day, generous enough that Film Ferret’s scores ask nothing of anyone.

3. Give feedback

Constructive feedback is not a suggestion box here; it is the development process. Two real channels, no form that mails into the void:

  • From the web: open an issue at github.com/garrisonr/vizsla-website/issues — bug reports, feature wishes, and beta sign-ups all live there, in the open.
  • If you run Vizsla: feedback rides the channel the system itself uses for every human decision — Pass or Paws, the decision inbox built into the OS.

What good feedback looks like here: “This screen made me stop and think.” “I looked for X in the obvious place and it wasn’t there.” “It did something I didn’t ask it to do” — the gravest charge in this house, and the fastest to get fixed.
